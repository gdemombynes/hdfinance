# Concept Note: Financing Human Capital Development — A Global Assessment

## World Bank Study Proposal

**Date:** March 2026

---

## 1. Background and Motivation

Human capital — the knowledge, skills, and health that people accumulate over their lives — is a critical driver of economic growth, poverty reduction, and shared prosperity. The World Bank's Human Capital Project, launched in 2018, catalyzed global attention to the urgency of investing in people. The Human Capital Index (HCI) revealed that a child born in a low-income country could expect to achieve only 40–50 percent of their productive potential by age 18, compared to 70–80 percent in high-income countries.

Closing these gaps requires adequate and effective financing across three interconnected domains: **education**, **health**, and **social protection**. Yet the global landscape of human capital financing remains fragmented, insufficiently understood, and poorly aligned with needs. The COVID-19 pandemic caused unprecedented disruptions to service delivery and fiscal pressures on government budgets, while the subsequent recovery has been uneven. Many low- and middle-income countries now face a "polycrisis" of constrained fiscal space, rising debt burdens, demographic pressures, and climate-related shocks — all of which threaten investments in people.

This study proposes a comprehensive assessment of the financing of human capital development across countries and over time, encompassing both public and private sources, to inform policy and investment decisions.

---

## 2. Objectives

The study aims to:

1. **Map the landscape** of human capital financing — education, health, and social protection — across countries and income groups, covering public expenditure, private spending, and external financing.
2. **Document trends** in financing levels, composition, and adequacy over the past two decades (2005–2025), with attention to the COVID-19 shock and recovery.
3. **Assess financing gaps** by comparing current spending levels to estimated needs and to benchmarks associated with better human capital outcomes.
4. **Analyze efficiency and equity** in how resources are allocated and used within and across sectors.
5. **Identify policy lessons** from countries that have successfully expanded or improved human capital financing.
6. **Propose a forward-looking framework** for prioritizing and financing human capital investments in the context of fiscal constraints.

## 3. Scope

### 3.1 Sectoral Coverage

| Domain | Key Expenditure Categories |
|--------|---------------------------|
| **Education** | Pre-primary, primary, secondary, tertiary, vocational/technical education; teacher compensation; infrastructure; learning materials; school feeding programs |
| **Health** | Primary health care, maternal and child health, immunization, nutrition, disease prevention and treatment, health workforce, health infrastructure |
| **Social Protection** | Cash transfers (conditional and unconditional), social insurance, public works programs, school feeding, disability and old-age pensions, labor market programs |

### 3.2 Financing Sources

- **Domestic public financing:** Government budget allocations (central and subnational), earmarked taxes, social insurance contributions
- **Domestic private financing:** Household out-of-pocket expenditure, employer spending, private insurance, philanthropy
- **External financing:** Official development assistance (ODA), multilateral lending (IDA/IBRD, regional development banks), global funds and vertical initiatives (Global Fund, Gavi, GPE), private international philanthropy

### 3.3 Geographic and Temporal Scope

- All World Bank member countries, with detailed analysis by income group (low-income, lower-middle-income, upper-middle-income, high-income) and region
- Time period: 2005–2025, with projections to 2030
- Special attention to IDA-eligible countries and fragile and conflict-affected states (FCS)

## 5. Summary of Financing Trends

### 5.1 Education

- **Global average public education spending** has hovered around **4.3–4.5% of GDP** over the past decade, but with wide variation: high-income countries average ~5% of GDP, while low-income countries average ~3.5%.
- As a share of total government expenditure, education spending averages approximately **14–15%** globally. Many low-income countries meet or exceed the benchmark of 15–20% of government expenditure, but low revenue bases mean absolute spending per student remains very low.
- **Per-pupil spending** in low-income countries is roughly **US$50–100 per year** at primary level, compared to **US$8,000–12,000** in high-income countries — a gap of two orders of magnitude.
- **Household out-of-pocket spending** on education is substantial in many developing countries, accounting for 30–50% of total education spending in parts of Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, creating equity concerns.
- **Aid to education** has stagnated in real terms; education receives only ~10% of total ODA despite being a stated priority.
- COVID-19 led to fiscal contractions that squeezed education budgets in many countries during 2020–2021, with incomplete recovery by 2023.

### 5.2 Health

- **Global current health expenditure** averages approximately **10% of GDP** across high-income countries but only **5–6%** in low- and middle-income countries and **~5%** in low-income countries.
- **Domestic general government health expenditure** averages about **3.5–4%** of GDP in upper-middle-income countries, **1.5–2%** in lower-middle-income countries, and around **1–1.5%** in low-income countries.
- **Out-of-pocket expenditure** remains high in many LMICs, exceeding 40% of current health expenditure in South Asia and parts of Sub-Saharan Africa, driving impoverishment.
- **External health expenditure** accounts for over 20% of current health expenditure in many low-income countries, reaching 30–50% in some fragile states, raising sustainability concerns.
- The COVID-19 pandemic triggered a surge in health spending in 2020–2021, followed by fiscal consolidation that has, in many countries, reduced health budgets below pre-pandemic trajectories.

### 5.3 Social Protection

- **Social protection spending** varies enormously: high-income countries spend an average of **15–20% of GDP** (including pensions), upper-middle-income countries spend **8–12%**, lower-middle-income countries spend **3–6%**, and low-income countries spend roughly **1–2% of GDP**.
- Excluding contributory pensions, **social safety net spending** in low-income countries averages about **1.5% of GDP** — well below the estimated 3–5% needed for adequate coverage.
- The COVID-19 crisis triggered an unprecedented expansion of social protection, with over 200 countries introducing new programs or expanding existing ones. However, most of these expansions were temporary, and many have since been wound down.
- **Coverage gaps** remain large: only about 47% of the global population is covered by at least one social protection benefit, dropping to less than 10% in many low-income countries.

### 5.4 Aggregate Human Capital Financing

- Combining education, health (public), and social protection spending, **total public human capital expenditure** in low-income countries averages roughly **6–9% of GDP**, compared to **25–30% of GDP** in high-income OECD countries.
- The **financing gap** — the difference between current spending and the levels associated with achieving universal access to basic services and the SDGs — has been estimated at **US$3–5 trillion per year** globally, with the largest relative gaps in Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia.
- **Fiscal space** for increasing HC spending is constrained by low tax-to-GDP ratios (averaging 10–15% in low-income countries versus 35–45% in high-income countries), rising debt burdens, and competing expenditure demands.

## 6. Key Research Questions

### 6.1 Levels and Adequacy

1. What are the current levels of public and private spending on education, health, and social protection by country and income group, and how have these evolved since 2005?
2. What are the financing gaps relative to (a) normative benchmarks (e.g., the 4–6% of GDP for education, 5% for health under the Abuja Declaration), (b) levels associated with achieving SDG targets, and (c) levels needed to reach the top quartile of the Human Capital Index?
3. How does the composition of financing (public vs. private vs. external) affect the equity and effectiveness of spending?

### 6.2 Trends and Shocks

4. How did the COVID-19 pandemic affect HC financing levels, composition, and efficiency, and to what extent has recovery occurred?
5. What role have debt sustainability pressures played in constraining HC investments, and how can countries protect HC spending during fiscal consolidation?
6. How have demographic transitions (youth bulges in Africa, aging in East Asia) shifted HC financing needs and priorities?

### 6.3 Efficiency and Allocation

7. What is the relationship between HC spending levels and outcomes (learning, health, human capital index scores), and what explains cross-country variation in the efficiency of HC spending?
8. How is HC spending distributed across income quintiles within countries (benefit incidence), and what determines pro-poor versus regressive allocation?
9. What is the optimal allocation of incremental resources across education, health, and social protection to maximize human capital accumulation?

### 6.4 Financing Strategies

10. What are the most promising strategies for expanding fiscal space for HC — revenue mobilization, expenditure reprioritization, efficiency gains, or innovative financing?
11. What role should private financing play, and how can governments regulate private spending to ensure equity?
12. How can external financing (ODA, MDB lending, global funds) be better aligned with national HC priorities and used more catalytically?
13. What are the roles of sub-national governments in HC financing, and how do intergovernmental fiscal transfers affect HC spending?

### 6.5 Cross-Cutting Issues

14. How do HC financing patterns differ in fragile and conflict-affected states, and what financing modalities work in these contexts?
15. What is the role of climate change adaptation financing in protecting human capital, and how can HC and climate finance be better integrated?
16. How can countries leverage technology and digital public infrastructure to improve the efficiency of HC service delivery and reduce costs?

## 7. Analytical Strategy

### Phase 1: Data Assembly and Harmonization (Months 1–4)

**Objective:** Build a comprehensive, harmonized cross-country panel dataset on HC financing.

- **Task 1.1:** Extract and clean education, health, and social protection spending data from WDI, GHED, UIS, ILO, ASPIRE, OECD, and IMF GFS databases. Harmonize definitions and ensure cross-dataset consistency.
- **Task 1.2:** Compile private/household spending data from Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS), Living Standards Measurement Surveys (LSMS), and household budget surveys where available.
- **Task 1.3:** Assemble external financing data from OECD CRS, Global Fund, Gavi, and GPE disbursement records.
- **Task 1.4:** Link financing data to outcome indicators (Human Capital Index, learning-adjusted years of school, under-5 mortality, stunting rates, etc.).
- **Task 1.5:** Construct a unified dataset covering ~150 countries for 2005–2025, with metadata on data quality and comparability.

**Deliverable:** Publicly accessible HC financing database and technical documentation.

### Phase 2: Descriptive Analysis and Benchmarking (Months 3–6)

**Objective:** Produce a comprehensive descriptive portrait of HC financing globally.

- **Task 2.1:** Generate country profiles showing levels and trends in HC spending by sector, source, and level of government.
- **Task 2.2:** Construct benchmarking analysis: compare country spending to (a) income-group averages, (b) normative targets, (c) spending levels of "HC over-performers" (countries with better outcomes than predicted by income).
- **Task 2.3:** Decompose changes over time into price, quantity, and composition effects.
- **Task 2.4:** Map the COVID-19 fiscal shock and recovery trajectory by country and sector.
- **Task 2.5:** Estimate aggregate financing gaps relative to SDG-consistent spending scenarios.

**Deliverable:** Global HC Financing Report — descriptive chapters with data visualizations and country dashboards.

### Phase 3: Analytical Deep Dives (Months 5–10)

**Objective:** Conduct rigorous analytical work on key questions.

- **Task 3.1: Spending-Outcome Nexus.** Estimate the relationship between HC spending and outcomes using panel regressions, stochastic frontier analysis, and data envelopment analysis (DEA) to assess efficiency. Control for governance quality, institutional capacity, and demographic factors.
- **Task 3.2: Equity Analysis.** Use household survey data to conduct benefit incidence analysis of education, health, and social protection spending in a sample of 30–40 countries. Assess progressivity and targeting effectiveness.
- **Task 3.3: Fiscal Space Analysis.** For a sample of 50 countries, assess the potential for expanding HC financing through (a) revenue mobilization, (b) expenditure reallocation, (c) efficiency gains, and (d) borrowing. Use the IMF/World Bank fiscal space framework.
- **Task 3.4: Optimal Allocation.** Develop a simulation model to assess the returns to incremental HC investments across sectors (education, health, social protection) and sub-sectors, drawing on cost-effectiveness evidence and the Human Capital Index methodology.
- **Task 3.5: Political Economy.** Conduct case studies (8–10 countries) examining the political economy of HC financing decisions — what enabled countries to sustain or increase HC investments during difficult periods.

**Deliverable:** Analytical working papers on each deep-dive topic.

### Phase 4: Synthesis and Policy Framework (Months 9–12)

**Objective:** Synthesize findings into an actionable policy framework.

- **Task 4.1:** Develop a "HC Financing Adequacy Framework" that provides country-specific guidance on target spending levels, financing strategies, and allocation priorities.
- **Task 4.2:** Produce a set of policy recommendations organized by country typology (income level, fiscal space, demographic profile, fragility status).
- **Task 4.3:** Design a forward-looking projection model: estimate HC financing needs and gaps through 2030 under different macroeconomic and policy scenarios.
- **Task 4.4:** Produce the final flagship report, policy briefs, and data tools.

**Deliverable:** Final report, policy briefs, interactive data platform, and projection tool.
